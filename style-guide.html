Tableau · Visual standards

A practical Tableau style guide

A reusable set of rules for building dashboards that look polished, read quickly, use color intentionally, remain accessible to color-blind viewers, and label charts so the viewer never has to guess what they're looking at.

01 · Make the insight obviousTitles should tell the viewer what the chart says, not simply repeat the field name.
02 · Design for color blindnessColor should reinforce meaning, never be the only way a viewer can distinguish categories.
03 · Label the data, not the interfaceUse direct labels, units, source notes, and useful tooltips instead of visual clutter.

01

Dashboard hierarchy

A dashboard should have a visual reading order. The viewer should know where to start, what matters most, and what action to take without being taught how to use the page.

LevelWhat belongs hereRule
1 · ContextDashboard title, subtitle, period, scopeAnswer “What am I looking at?” in one glance.
2 · AnswerKPI cards, headline chart, key comparisonPut the most important takeaway above the fold.
3 · ExplainTrends, breakdowns, drivers, distributionsUse supporting views to explain why the headline moved.
4 · ExploreFilters, details, tooltips, tablesKeep exploration available without making it the visual focus.
Do

Use whitespace to separate ideas. Align chart edges and keep a consistent left edge for titles and text.

Don't

Give every chart the same visual weight. A dashboard with eight “most important” charts has no hierarchy.

02

Titles & chart labeling

This is one of the biggest upgrades to make to a Tableau portfolio: write titles as statements or questions that communicate the analytical point.

Weak

Sales by Region
Technically correct, but it makes the viewer do the interpretation.

Stronger

West leads sales, while the Central region trails by 18%
The chart title becomes part of the story.

Title formula

ElementRecommended approachExample
HeadlineInsight + subject“Revenue growth accelerated in Q3”
SubtitleTime period + scope + important definition“FY2026 through Q3 · U.S. business · YoY change”
X-axisName the dimension and use readable values“Month” rather than “Order Date”
Y-axisName the measure and unit“Revenue ($M)” rather than “SUM(Revenue)”
LegendDescribe the encoding“Product category” rather than “Color”
Source noteSource + date when relevant“Source: Finance data · refreshed Sep 2026”
TABLEAU

In Tableau, use Edit Title and Edit Axis rather than accepting raw field names. Tableau supports custom axis titles and subtitles, and titles can include dynamic field values. Tableau: Edit Axes.

04

Chart selection + labeling examples

Choose the chart based on the analytical question first. Then remove anything that doesn't help answer that question.

Compare

Sorted horizontal bar

Best for rankings, categories, and long labels. Put the category on the vertical axis and sort by the measure.

Trend

Line chart + direct end labels

For time series. Prefer direct labels at the line end when there are only a few series instead of making the viewer bounce to a legend.

Relationship

Scatter plot + reference line

Use for two quantitative variables. Label notable outliers rather than every mark.

Composition

100% stacked bar

Use when the question is how a whole is composed across categories. Label the segments that matter; don't label every tiny slice.

Bullet chart

A strong alternative to gauges. Shows actual, target, and performance band without wasting space on a dial.

Small multiples

When one chart becomes a spaghetti plot, split it into repeated mini-views with the same scale and consistent encoding.

Quick picker

QuestionDefaultLabeling rule
Which is bigger?Sorted barDirect value labels when there are fewer than ~10 marks.

05

Axes, numbers & scales

A polished dashboard can still mislead if the axis, unit, or number format is unclear. Formatting is part of the analysis.

Do

Use $12.4M, 84.2%, or 1.3K when precision doesn't matter. State the unit in the axis or subtitle.

Don't

Mix $ / dollars / USD / $M in the same view or show six decimal places for a metric reported to executives.

RuleGuidance
Bar baselineStart at zero for magnitude comparisons unless there is a compelling analytical reason not to.
Line scaleChoose a range that preserves meaningful movement; avoid dramatic visual exaggeration.
Dual axisUse only when the comparison is genuinely useful. Clearly label both measures and avoid implying equal units.
PercentShow percentage values as percentages, not raw decimals. Keep decimal precision consistent.
DatesUse readable labels such as Jan, Q1 2026, or FY2026 rather than long timestamp strings.
GridlinesKeep them subtle and use only enough to help estimate values.

ElementStandard
NumberLargest element. Use appropriate abbreviation and precision.
LabelShort, specific, and understandable without the chart.
ComparisonUse vs. prior period, target, or benchmark, not all three.
StatusPair color with text/icon. “▲ 8.4%” is more accessible than a green number alone.

07

Tooltips, annotations & interaction

Interactivity should reveal detail, not hide the answer. Use it to progressively disclose information.

Tooltip

Make it a mini-story

Include the category, formatted value, relevant comparison, and definition. Avoid dumping every field in the data source.

Annotation

Explain the unusual

Annotate spikes, drops, launches, policy changes, or outliers when the event helps explain the data.

Highlight

Focus attention

Use highlight actions when the category is already visible. Avoid adding a filter control for every interaction.

Drill-down

Progressive detail

Keep the first view simple, then allow users to move from summary → driver → record-level detail.

ACCESS

Tableau notes that interactive elements such as mark selection and tooltips are not themselves accessible to every user. Provide useful titles, captions, legends, filters, and text equivalents so the core message does not depend on hover. Tableau: Author Views for Accessibility.

08

Filters & dashboard controls

Filters should be predictable, compact, and limited to decisions the viewer actually needs to make.

ControlUse whenDesign standard
Global filter bar1–3 filters affect most viewsKeep in the same location across the workbook.
Left filter railMany filters or long dashboardsGroup related filters and give the rail a clear heading.
ParameterUser changes a metric, scenario, or viewUse for analysis choices, not simply to recreate a standard filter.
Dashboard actionThe category is already visibleClick-to-filter/highlight is often cleaner than another control.
ResetMultiple interactive controlsProvide an obvious reset path when users can create complex states.

09

Accessibility shipping checklist

Run this checklist before publishing. Tableau's current accessibility guidance aligns its accessible-view recommendations with WCAG 2.2 AA principles. Tableau: Build Data Views for Accessibility.

□ Color is not the only way to distinguish important marks.
□ Categorical dimensions use a color-blind-friendly palette.
□ Important lines use shape, labels, or another visual cue.
□ Text has sufficient contrast against its background.
□ Every chart has a descriptive title.
□ Axes include meaningful names and units.
□ Legends explain what the encoding represents.
□ Tooltips add context rather than duplicate raw fields.
□ The dashboard still makes sense without hovering.
□ Filters have clear labels and predictable scope.
□ The reading order is logical for assistive technology.
□ View Data / text alternatives are available where appropriate.

10

Final design rules

01

Lead with the answer. A chart title should help someone understand the takeaway before they inspect the marks.

02

Use color intentionally. One meaning per color, consistent across the workbook.

03

Label what matters. Direct-label the important values and remove unnecessary legends.

04

Prefer simple charts. A well-designed bar, line, or scatter plot beats a clever chart that takes explaining.

05

Design for the least obvious viewer. If someone can't distinguish the colors or understand the title, the visualization is not finished.

06

Remove before adding. Every border, label, color, filter, and decoration should earn its place.
