A Tableau Style Guide

Working conventions for banners, filter placement, and color — the same rules I used to build the dashboards in this portfolio, written down so they're consistent next time.

01

Banners & title bars

The banner's only job is to orient someone in under two seconds: what is this, and what period or scope am I looking at. Everything past that is competing for attention it doesn't need.

Measures

Title, one line of context, and at most two live stats. No filters live here.

Do

Keep it to a title, one line of context (data source, date range, scope), and — if there's room — one or two live stat chips.

Don't

Don't put filters, legends, or a chart inside the banner. If it needs a scroll to find, it's not a banner anymore.

Do

Cap it around 8–10% of a 1080p viewport (roughly 80–110px). Build it as a floating container pinned to the top with a colored rectangle behind it.

Don't

Don't let the banner grow taller than the tallest chart below it — it should announce the page, not dominate it.

02

Where filters go

Filter placement is a decision, not a default. Pick based on how many filters there are and whether they apply to the whole page or just one view.

Top bar — 1 to 3 global filters
Best when people scan left-to-right first and the filters apply to everything below. This is what both dashboards in this portfolio use.
Left rail — 4+ filters, many tabs
Best when filters need to stay visible while scrolling a long page, or across multiple dashboard tabs in one workbook.

In-visual (quick) filters

Only for a filter that's local to one specific chart — not the whole page. If more than one visual needs it, promote it to the top bar or rail instead; a filter that only half-applies is the single most common source of "wait, why didn't that update?" confusion.

Click-to-filter actions

Prefer a dashboard action (click a bar to filter or highlight the rest of the page) over adding another filter widget, when the category is already visible in a chart. It's one less control competing for space, and it teaches itself — people click marks instinctively.

RULE

Filters live in the same place on every dashboard in a workbook. Moving them page to page forces a re-scan every single time.

03

Color

Color is the fastest channel a viewer reads — which means it's also the fastest way to mislead them if it's inconsistent.

Categorical (distinct groups)

Cap it at 6–8 hues before a legend stops being readable. One signature color for "the" category that matters, neutral gray for everything else.

Sequential (low → high, one measure)

A single hue, light to dark. This is what conditional-formatting data bars and heat tables should use — never a rainbow.

Diverging (below / above a midpoint)

Two hues moving away from a neutral center — for things like correlation, variance to target, or year-over-year change.

Do

Keep one color meaning one thing across every chart on the page. If red means "Red wine" in the scatter plot, it means the same thing in every other chart on that page.

Don't

Don't fully hide marks when cross-filtering — dim them to ~10–20% opacity instead, so the viewer keeps the context of what's being excluded.

CHECK

Run the palette through a color-blindness simulator before shipping. Red/green as the only signal fails for a meaningful share of viewers — pair color with position, label, or icon wherever the distinction actually matters.

04

KPI cards

One number, one label. The temptation is always to fit three metrics into one card — resist it.

ElementGuidance
Primary numberLargest text on the card. Tabular/monospaced figures so multiple cards align.
LabelSmall, uppercase, muted — sits above or below the number, never competes with it in size.
ComparisonOptional second line only — vs. last period or vs. target. If it doesn't change the takeaway, leave it off.
Conditional colorColor the number itself against a threshold, not the whole card background — a card that's fully red reads as an error state, not a data point.

05

Chart picker

A quick reference for matching the question to the chart, before defaulting to a bar chart for everything.

QuestionChartNotes
Compare categoriesBar chartHorizontal when labels are long; sort by value, not alphabetically.
Trend over timeLine chartOne line per category, max 4–5 before it's noise. Consider small multiples past that.
Relationship between two measuresScatter plotAdd a third measure as point size, and color for a category, before adding a fourth axis.
Part-to-wholeStacked bar or donutDonut only past 2–3 slices makes it hard to compare; a stacked bar reads more precisely.
Distribution / spreadHistogram or box plotHistogram for one group, box plot to compare spread across several groups at once.
RankingSorted barAlways sorted — an unsorted ranking chart isn't actually showing rank.
Row-level detailTableAdd data bars or a color scale on the one column that matters most — not every column.
